# Jellinek's odor effects for the profiler

Source: Paul Jellinek, *The Psychological Basis of Perfumery*, ed. J. Stephan Jellinek (1997). Part I is the 1951 text; Part II adds later chapters and the editor's re-evaluation. Pages are printed pages (file marker = page + 14 in Part I, + 13 in Part II). Family weights: `jellinek_mapping.json`.

## (a) Jellinek's system

**Purpose.** Jellinek holds that perfumery serves sexual attraction by masking unpleasant body odors and adding materials with an erotic effect (p. 3), so a true perfume needs an erotic base (p. 8). He separates odors (rosy, woody) from effects (calming, exciting), which are reactions to odors (p. 90).

**Four effects on two axes.**
- Erogenous: arousing sexual desire (p. 9). Carried by materials whose odor recalls the human body (animal extracts, fatty aldehydes, indol), and only at low concentration and alongside other pleasant impressions (pp. 32-45).
- Anti-erogenous, or refreshing: its opposite. Volatile, short-lived materials that mask body odor (pp. 4-6, 52).
- Narcotic: "soft" floral and balsamic odors that dull critical thought and make the smeller more receptive to feelings (pp. 49-51).
- Stimulating: "hard", spicy or sharp odors that animate and raise activity. He links hard and soft to male and female (pp. 51-52).

Adjacent effects combine into four composites: fresh (refreshing + stimulating), exalting (stimulating + erogenous), sultry (erogenous + narcotic) and calming (narcotic + refreshing) (pp. 48, 55, 89, 105).

**The diagram.** The Odor Effects Diagram is a square, not a hexagon: refreshing at the top, stimulating right, erogenous bottom, narcotic left, composites on the sides (pp. 53, 89). He derived it from the components of jasmine (p. 52). Opposite positions are contrasting effects, which the perfumer uses to strengthen or soften an effect (p. 89). A complete perfume contains all four effects; its overall "focal point" cannot be calculated and must be judged on the finished perfume (pp. 72, 153, 157-158). For matching perfumes to people he scores positions numerically, erogenous and narcotic positive, refreshing and stimulating negative (pp. 122-123).

**Classifying materials.** His only reported test: he and two perfumers compared a base accord with and without an added material and judged which was more erotic (pp. 11-17). Results depended on the base accord (p. 18), and the judges contradicted each other on cassie, oak moss, orange flower, lavender oil, coumarin and vetiveryl acetate (p. 21). The Chapter 18 list, built from years of observation and stated to be neither absolute nor final (pp. 143, 146), gives this picture: citrus, conifer and camphor oils are refreshing; spices, coumarin, vanillin, vetiver, patchouli and oak moss stimulating; the fatty aldehydes exalting; animal extracts, fatty alcohols and indol erogenous; jasmine, orange flower, labdanum and tolu sultry; rose, the floral alcohols and synthetic musks narcotic; lavender absolute and linalyl acetate calming (pp. 144-148). Effect follows the chemical functional group more than the odor family (p. 149).

**Preference.** Nineteenth-century taste favored narcotic perfumes; modern taste wants erogenous plus stimulating, which he calls exalting (pp. 86, 88-89, 99).

**Personality, sex and age.** He warns that inferring a woman's character from her perfume is risky (p. 110). He then proposes choosing a woman's perfume from four "type diagrams": hair color (blonde fresh, redhead exalting, brunette sultry, brown-haired calming), mother versus mistress, feminine (narcotic) versus masculine (stimulating), and naive (refreshing) versus artful (erogenous) (pp. 114-118, 123). Only her displayed manner counts, not her inner psyche (p. 118). He bases this on observing friends and acquaintances (p. 114); the hair types rest on his claim that blondes, redheads and brunettes smell different (pp. 28-29). Age: girls and older women suit fresh or calming perfumes; women from about 20 to 50 can wear all erogenous types (pp. 118-119). Men must avoid erogenous materials and strong narcotic effects; Eau de Cologne, lavender and Fougere without animal notes suit them (pp. 127-129).

**Where the editor qualifies or updates.** J. Stephan Jellinek calls Part I a historic document (p. vii) and tests each thesis (pp. 240-256):
- Universality: behaviorist research treats odor liking as learned (p. 242). Effects should be assumed to hold only where observed; some probably do not apply in Arab or East Asian cultures (p. 243).
- Context and concentration dependence: confirmed (pp. 243-244).
- Mixture effects as the sum of component effects: two studies, both of doubtful significance (p. 244); situation, prior learning and components all contribute (p. 245).
- The diagram: the effects of individual materials remain unproven physiologically, but consumer studies of commercial perfumes repeatedly produce two dimensions close to his (heavy-light, floral-non-floral, sensual-cool), valid for Euro-American consumers (pp. 246-248).
- Body-like means erogenous: plausible that such materials recall the body, less plausible that the association is erotic; he limits the claim to odors of the urogenital and anal regions (pp. 249-250).
- Purpose: the sexual-drive thesis is no longer tenable (p. 251). In a 1992 survey of 317 female US students, 73% used perfume to feel good about themselves and 35% to attract men (p. 251).
- Musks: synthetic musks are narcotic and not perceived as body-like, so the rule that every perfume needs an erogenous base no longer necessarily holds (pp. 253-254).
- Personal type: most users now own several perfumes and switch by mood and occasion; Jellinek's own list put nearly every fashion perfume of his day at "exalting", which the editor calls embarrassing (p. 255).
- Men: the rule has largely lost validity, and Islamic and Pacific cultures never separated masculine and feminine scents (p. 256).
- Closing judgment: the observations are "valid only for the culture and the epoch in which they were conceived" (p. 256).

**Contradicting research in Part II.** Steiner found no difference in pupil dilation between odors (pp. 204-205), activation rankings that tracked odor intensity (p. 209), physiological and self-reported activation correlating only at 0.50 (p. 214), and results opposite to Torii's for bergamot, rose and ylang-ylang (p. 216). Dollase reports that like-dislike is the only rating dimension people share, other dimensions being individual (p. 221), and that people choose perfume by their own liking because they cannot observe its effect on others (p. 224). Bain finds odor liking largely learned and perfume motives varied (pp. 233, 238).

## (b) Critical assessment

**Usable today as descriptions of taste.**
1. The two axes as a vocabulary for groups of materials: animalic and heavy versus clean and refreshing; soft floral-balsamic versus sharp, spicy or dry. Consumer studies support these as dimensions along which people sort perfumes, not as effects on people (pp. 246-248).
2. The Chapter 18 placements as a coarse grouping of materials.
3. Odor versus effect, and dependence on context and concentration (pp. 18, 90, 244).
4. Situational use of several perfumes (pp. 119, 255).

**Dated or unsupported.**
1. The sexual purpose of perfume, and the claim that materials act on the smeller. Not shown physiologically (p. 248); measurement methods disagree (pp. 209, 214-216).
2. The evidence base: three judges, averaged scores, no statistics, frequent disagreement (pp. 11-21).
3. Additivity. The proposal below averages family weights, which assumes what the editor calls unproven (p. 244).
4. Universality. The site serves Arabic speakers, the editor expects some placements not to hold in Arab culture (p. 243), and the site's two oud families have no counterpart in the book.
5. The type diagrams (hair color, mother/mistress, naive/artful, feminine/masculine, age brackets), for the reasons given in (a). Discard them entirely.
6. The rule that men should avoid erogenous notes (p. 256).
7. The 1940s palette: 6 of the site's 28 families are not covered (woody ambers, both ouds, aquatic, coffee, saffron), and white musk, cedar, fruity and tobacco are only partly covered.
8. People agree only on like-dislike (p. 221), so any effect label is an interpretation.

**The site must not claim** that the profile reveals personality, temperament, sexuality, femininity or masculinity, life stage or appearance; that any note arouses, calms or energizes the wearer or others; that certain effects suit men, women or particular ages; that the labels are measured or validated; or that they apply equally to Arab users. In user-facing text, replace "erogenous", "sultry" and "narcotic" with the plain `display_en` labels from the JSON.

## (c) Proposal: an effect profile

**Computation.**
1. For each rated stage s, take the centered rating r_s from -1 (strong dislike) to +1 (strong like) and the stage's family weights m_s,f, the same ones the dislike inference uses. Drop unmapped families and `incense_resin` (confidence "conflicting") and renormalize. Skip a stage if less than half of its weight remains.
2. Stage effect vector: v_s,k = sum over f of m_s,f × w_f,k, for the four primary effects k. Multiply by a confidence factor (high 1.0, medium 0.75, low 0.5).
3. Effect score S_k = sum over s of (r_s × v_s,k) / sum over s of v_s,k: the person's average rating of what they smelled, weighted by how much of effect k each stage contained. Exposure X_k = sum over s of v_s,k.
4. Report an effect only if X_k reaches a minimum (start at 1.5 stage-equivalents). Refreshing and erogenous dominate only three families each, against seven or eight for the other two, so they will often fall short.
5. Use the axes of Jellinek's calculation diagram (p. 122): E = S_erogenous − S_anti_erogenous and N = S_narcotic − S_stimulating. If the length of (E, N) is below a threshold τ, report no clear leaning, as Jellinek does when type scores sum to zero (pp. 123-124). Otherwise name the nearest of the eight positions, and the lowest-scoring effect if its S_k is below −τ.
6. Set X_min and τ with synthetic raters: one who dislikes only `animalic` must come out rejecting animalic notes and nothing else; random raters must mostly get "no clear leaning".

The profile re-describes the family ratings and adds no information; two families can lower the same effect for different reasons (soapy aldehydes, fecal civet), and the label merges them.

**Example output.**
> You gave your lowest ratings to animalic and skin-like notes (civet, honey, waxy aldehydes) and your highest to refreshing and sharp ones (citrus, herbs, spices, dry woods). In the scheme the perfumer Paul Jellinek proposed in 1951, that places your taste in his "fresh" group and away from his animalic group. This describes the materials you rated, not you.

**Evidential status (text for the page).**
> These labels come from Paul Jellinek's 1951 classification of perfume materials. His placements came from his own and two colleagues' judgments, not from controlled tests. Later consumer studies, mostly in Europe and North America, found similar broad groupings of perfumes, but no study has shown that the materials have the effects his names suggest, and the groupings may fit Arab and Gulf users less well. We use them only to describe taste.
